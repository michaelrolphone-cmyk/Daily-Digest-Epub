## Physics · Ultrafast solid-state optics — Why electrons lose phase coherence in four femtoseconds when a semiconductor emits harmonics

Wavelength-dependent emission from monolayer semiconductors exposes the difference between a fitted relaxation time and a momentum-resolved scattering mechanism.

Korolev, Lettau, Krishna and collaborators; Nature Communications 17, article 10954; peer-reviewed open-access study published 20 October 2026, DOI 10.1038/s41467-026-78716-1.

High-order harmonic generation begins with an intense, relatively low-frequency laser and ends with much higher-frequency light. In a semiconductor the drive can promote electrons across a band gap and accelerate them through the periodic crystal. A moving electron and the corresponding valence-band hole form an oscillating polarization; radiation emitted by that polarization contains multiples of the original drive frequency. The difficult part is preserving a definite relative phase long enough for contributions from different paths to interfere constructively. A phenomenological simulation can force agreement with measured spectra by making the polarization forget its phase in a few femtoseconds. That fits an observation, but says little about which microscopic process destroyed the coherence. [1]

Viacheslav Korolev, Thomas Lettau, Vipin Krishna and a multidisciplinary team addressed this problem by changing the mid-infrared drive wavelength and watching how rapidly the above-band-gap harmonic yield disappeared. They studied mechanically exfoliated monolayer tungsten disulfide (WS2) and molybdenum disulfide (MoS2), transferred to sapphire, and a 140-nanometer-thick polycrystalline CdSe layer grown on sapphire. The monolayers help separate microscopic dynamics from propagation and phase-matching effects through bulk material. The CdSe comparison tests whether the measured dependence is peculiar to one two-dimensional crystal. It is not a direct pump–probe clock measurement of an individual electron; the observable is emitted optical power integrated over a defined spectral window. [1]

The experimental chain matters. A regenerative amplifier delivered 800-nanometer, 35-femtosecond pulses at 1 kilohertz. Parametric conversion and difference-frequency generation supplied pulses about 60–100 femtoseconds long in the mid-infrared. For the wavelength scan the investigators used excitation between 3 and 5 micrometers and an approximately fixed peak intensity of 1.45 terawatts per square centimeter. They characterized pulse duration by second-harmonic frequency-resolved optical gating, imaged the beam profile, and interpolated measured yield against intensity to avoid mistaking a changing focal intensity for a wavelength effect. A UV-capable spectrometer registered harmonic output in approximately the 200–1100-nanometer range. Detector response was corrected before integration of photons with energy above the material band gap. [1]

They fitted the integrated yield to I(lambda) proportional to lambda^(-x), with lambda the driving wavelength and x the positive decay exponent. For WS2 they found x = 8.9 ± 1.0 along a crystallographic zigzag direction and 8.2 ± 0.9 along armchair. MoS2 gave x = 6.6 ± 2.0 and 6.2 ± 1.4 for those orientations; CdSe also gave approximately 6.2 ± 1.4. These are uncertainties on fitted slopes, not claimed percentages of device efficiency. The harmonic power also oscillated around the trend because interfering quantum trajectories have wavelength-dependent phase. A simple model with no explicit decoherence predicted a much gentler fall, x about 3.4 in semiconductor Bloch calculations and 3.3 in real-time time-dependent density-functional calculations. [1]

Here is a derived scale comparison, not a new measurement. If a smooth law with x = 8 described the full interval, moving the pump from 3 to 5 micrometers would multiply integrated yield by (5/3)^(-8), about 0.017: nearly sixtyfold less. A no-dephasing x = 3.4 law would give about 0.176, only a 5.7-fold reduction. The difference makes a slope a sensitive diagnostic of microscopic scattering. Real data have interference oscillations and detector windows, so one must not mistake those illustrative ratios for separately observed yield points. [1]

A tempting shortcut is to insert a constant polarization coherence time T2 into the Bloch equations. A value around 5 femtoseconds can reproduce the steep wavelength dependence. But it fails a second test: at a representative 4.2-micrometer drive it suppresses the high-order spectral plateau relative to low orders by roughly two orders of magnitude. The experiment does not exhibit this wholesale disappearance of plateau harmonics. A single scalar parameter therefore repairs the slope while breaking the spectral shape. This is a general scientific warning: matching one aggregate observable does not imply that the underlying dynamical mechanism is right. [1]

The team's alternative derives electron–phonon scattering rates from electronic and lattice structure. Density-functional calculations in Quantum Espresso, lattice-vibration calculations with density-functional perturbation theory, and Wannier-based scattering analysis provide separate carrier lifetimes tau for individual bands and crystal momenta k. The polarization between valence band m and conduction band n then acquires a momentum-dependent dephasing time: 1/T2(m,n,k) = [1/tau_m(k) + 1/tau_n(k)]/2. It is a relation between carrier scattering and interband polarization decay; it is not a single fitted lifetime. The calculated map is physically inhomogeneous across the Brillouin zone, which is the repeating cell of the crystal's momentum space. [1]

Close to the optically accessed K valley in WS2 the model yields T2 about 70 femtoseconds. It falls below 10 femtoseconds as carriers move away and approaches about 4 femtoseconds near the Gamma point. The strong laser field accelerates carriers away from their excitation location, so a path that begins in a relatively coherent valley spends time in strongly scattering regions. Inserting this momentum-resolved map into the semiconductor Bloch equations gives exponents 7.3 ± 0.2 for zigzag and 7.2 ± 0.4 for armchair, substantially closer to measured slopes while retaining a realistic harmonic plateau. A few-femtosecond effective coherence time emerges from moving across different scattering environments; the K valley itself need not intrinsically lose phase in four femtoseconds. [1]

The intensity dependence adds a discrimination test. The fitted exponent changed by less than 10% as the laser intensity was varied by more than 70%. The authors argue that if carrier–carrier collisions dominated here, the associated change in excited-carrier density, estimated near an order of magnitude, would have altered dephasing more strongly. That argument does not prove electron–electron collisions never matter. Their relative importance can increase under deliberate photodoping or still stronger fields. The electron–phonon rates were also computed in a field-free perturbative model; the experiment involves dynamically driven bands, nonthermal populations and possible pure dephasing. These are explicit approximations behind the quantitative match. [1]

The transferable result is a way to interrogate an ultrafast many-body process without directly resolving every scattering event. Change a drive parameter that changes the paths explored in momentum space, measure a robust integrated response, and challenge competing models with an independent observable such as spectral plateau height. Because the model predicts longer scattering times when phonons are suppressed, material temperature or engineered phonon spectra become physically motivated control variables. Those are research implications, not demonstrated high-efficiency quantum-light devices. What the experiment establishes now is evidence that the steep wavelength scaling comes largely from momentum-selective electron–phonon interactions in the tested semiconductors and field regime. [1]

## Physics · Precision sensing — An optically suspended particle resolves the kick from one gas-molecule collision

Impulse spectra provide information about both partial pressure and surface scattering, even when the gas is extremely dilute.

Yu-Han Tseng, Clarke A. Hardy, T. W. Penny and colleagues, Physical Review Letters 137, 173601; peer-reviewed experiment published 19 October 2026, DOI 10.1103/xtzh-6t1f. Full article subscription-restricted; journal abstract and APS Physics commentary consulted, not detailed supplementary methods.

Pressure gauges generally infer how many gas particles are present from a collective quantity: ion current, thermal conductivity, deformation of a diaphragm or a resonant response. As a gas becomes extremely rarefied, a different possibility appears. A suspended test mass is no longer pushed by effectively continuous bombardment; it receives occasional, potentially resolvable individual impulses. The measurement then resembles a particle counter, but the registered variable is mechanical momentum rather than charge or a light pulse. This is particularly attractive when a conventional pressure gauge changes or contaminates the environment it is supposed to diagnose. [1] [2]

Yu-Han Tseng, Clarke Hardy and collaborators at Yale report a proof-of-principle experiment in which an optically levitated nanoparticle registers momentum transfers from krypton atoms, xenon atoms and sulfur hexafluoride molecules. These were separate collision species, not a claim to chemically identify an arbitrary mixed gas from one unlabeled collision. The particle's position is tracked optically; a collision changes its motion, and the measured trajectory allows an impulse to be reconstructed. The journal reports impulses as small as 200 kilo-electron-volts divided by c, where c is the speed of light. An impulse carries momentum units. It is not a claim that the colliding molecule deposited 200 keV of kinetic energy. [1] [2]

For an idealized gas at temperature T and particle density n, pressure is p = n k_B T, with k_B Boltzmann's constant. A crude event-rate model is R = n sigma v_bar: R is collisions per second, sigma an effective scattering cross-section and v_bar the mean relative speed. Thus a calibrated collision count can constrain gas density and partial pressure. In a real levitated sensor, geometry, particle size, molecular speed distribution and a finite impulse-detection threshold enter the rate model. A pressure estimate derived from observed events therefore depends on how many true impacts remain below the detector's trigger sensitivity. This equation is an illustrative model, not the authors' complete calibration formula. [1]

Counting events answers only part of the question. Consider two gas molecules that strike the same surface with comparable incoming momentum but leave in different directions, or accommodate different amounts of energy on the surface. Their net impulses need not be identical. The distribution of impulse magnitudes consequently contains information about gas–surface energy and momentum accommodation and can constrain the nanoparticle's surface temperature. The paper's abstract says the spectral shape is sensitive to surface properties, including temperature, rather than giving a universal single-collision thermometer. It also says the observed rate agrees with theoretical expectations and measures partial pressures. Exact uncertainty bars for that pressure comparison are not available in the accessible abstract, so none are supplied here. [1] [2]

The distinction between detection threshold and bulk sensitivity is important. A minimum reconstructed impulse of 200 keV/c establishes that the optical mechanical readout can resolve remarkably weak isolated kicks. It does not, by itself, specify the lowest pressure measurable in a fixed integration time. At lower pressure the mean wait for a collision gets longer, and even a perfect sensor must accumulate enough events for a useful statistical uncertainty. If N independent counts are approximately Poisson distributed, the fractional counting uncertainty is roughly 1/sqrt(N). That is a derived counting illustration: one hundred accepted collisions still imply about ten percent counting noise before calibration uncertainty. [1]

The primary result is experimental individual-collision detection from three identified gas species, with momentum and rate statistics compatible with the proposed optomechanical model. It supports a path toward pressure measurement without ionizing the measured gas and toward probes of surface accommodation at microscopic scale. The full PRL text was not accessible here, so the instrument's trapping laser powers, particle-calibration error budget, acquisition durations, fitted surface temperatures and systematic uncertainties should not be reconstructed from guesswork. No evidence in the accessible material supports calling this a finished field-deployable gauge, a general mass spectrometer or a direct observation of dark-matter impacts. [1] [2]

## Physics · Quantum optomechanics — A room-temperature levitated oscillator shares entanglement with departing light

The experiment reconstructs joint optical–mechanical correlations, establishing a nonclassical link beyond the cavity.

Q. Deplano, A. Pontin, F. Marino and F. Marin; Science 394, 113–117; peer-reviewed publication 1 October 2026, DOI 10.1126/science.aeh1375. Earlier arXiv:2602.03456 first posted 3 February 2026; journal abstract and updated preprint abstract were accessible, full methods not established here.

A classical vibrating sphere and a bright optical beam can be strongly correlated without being entangled. The sphere moves, shifts the phase of scattered light, and a detector reads out that phase; ordinary common-cause dynamics explain the correlation. The harder task is to show that the combined quantum state of the mechanical motion and the outgoing light cannot be expressed as a mixture of independent states. That asks for a separability test rather than merely an image of a wobbling bead. It matters because an optical field can leave the laboratory apparatus while a mechanical object remains trapped, providing two physically different carriers of a quantum state. [1] [2]

Q. Deplano, A. Pontin, F. Marino and F. Marin investigated a nanosphere levitated by an optical tweezer inside an optical cavity. Coherent scattering connects the sphere's center-of-mass motion to an intracavity optical mode. The experiment uses optical readout of light escaping the apparatus, rather than a mechanical electrical contact that would spoil levitation. The team reports generating stationary optomechanical entanglement and observing a violation of separability bounds from reconstructed quadrature correlations. The qualifier stationary means the coupling produces correlations in a sustained operating regime, not merely a fleeting heralded photon pair. [1] [2]

Quadratures are complementary real variables used to specify a wave or oscillator, analogous to cosine and sine components of an electrical waveform. For the mechanical degree of freedom they correspond to appropriately normalized position and momentum. For light they correspond to amplitude- and phase-like field components. A heterodyne receiver measures an optical signal against a frequency-shifted reference, recording information from both optical quadratures. By examining how those measurements co-vary with the mechanical oscillation, the researchers reconstruct a covariance description and test whether any separable state could reproduce it. A violation of a valid bound is stronger evidence than finding a large ordinary correlation coefficient. [1] [2]

The work reports that the nonclassical correlations survive in propagating light outside the cavity and that the entanglement remains observable across a broad range of cavity detunings. The surrounding apparatus operated at room temperature. That does not mean every mechanical mode was thermally unperturbed: optical trapping and cooling are part of achieving a quantum-compatible state in an otherwise warm laboratory. The journal article appeared on 1 October, while its preprint dates back to February and was updated during the year. The appropriate news event is peer-reviewed publication this month, not invention of the result overnight. [1] [2]

The accessible publication abstract does not specify enough information to reconstruct the numerical covariance matrix, the exact separability-witness value with uncertainty or a frequency-by-frequency noise budget. Accordingly no invented entanglement negativity, decoherence time or communication distance belongs in this account. The demonstrated system is also not a network with two remote mechanical nodes. It establishes quantum correlation between one trapped object and departing light under measured conditions. Linking two such nodes, transmitting the correlations through lossy channels and certifying entanglement between distant mechanical objects remain separate technical tasks. [1] [2]

One conceptual consequence is that the experiment can be compared directly with single-molecule impulse detection from the same broad field of levitated optomechanics. Both exploit optical readout of motion without an attached mechanical support. In the collision experiment the desired signal is an individual classical momentum kick; here the important signal is a joint optical–mechanical quantum correlation that exceeds a classical separability bound. Confusing high force sensitivity with proof of entanglement would erase the central scientific distinction. The common hardware idea is suspension plus low-noise light-based measurement; the inference problem determines what has actually been demonstrated. [1] [2]

## Technology · Fault-tolerant quantum computing — Growing a fault-tolerant magic state instead of distilling a large batch

A superconducting processor demonstrates a high-quality non-Clifford resource at the cost of discarding most attempts.

Emma Rosenfeld, Craig Gidney, Gabrielle Roberts and the Google Quantum AI collaboration; Science, published 15 October 2026, DOI 10.1126/science.aee7961; earlier arXiv:2512.13908 first posted 15 December 2025. Peer-reviewed journal abstract and authors' preprint record consulted.

Quantum error correction is not yet the same as universal quantum computing. Many stabilizer codes make Clifford operations, such as CNOT and Hadamard, comparatively natural: they transform the algebra of measurable Pauli errors into itself. Clifford operations alone, even protected by a surface code, cannot implement arbitrary quantum algorithms efficiently. A non-Clifford T rotation supplies an essential missing resource but is difficult to enact fault-tolerantly as a direct physical operation. The established alternative is to prepare a special ancillary 'magic' quantum state and consume it to teleport the desired logical operation into a computation. Preparing such states accurately and frequently can dominate a fault-tolerant machine's space and time requirements. [1] [2]

A conventional magic-state distillation factory begins with many imperfect copies and uses a large error-detecting circuit to produce fewer cleaner copies. Its merits are understood mathematically, but factories require logical qubits, repeated operations and substantial routing. Magic-state cultivation changes the growth path: rather than first construct a large distance-protected factory and run repeated distillation rounds, it starts with an injected non-stabilizer state in a small code, repeatedly checks error syndromes, rejects suspicious trials and expands surviving states into a larger protective code. Postselection trades acceptance probability for conditional quality. A run with an error indication can be discarded rather than being treated as a successful output. [1] [2]

Emma Rosenfeld, Craig Gidney, Gabrielle Roberts and their extensive collaboration implemented this approach on a superconducting quantum processor. Their experiment includes code switching into a surface code, not just preparing and measuring an unencoded physical qubit. The methods also require fault-tolerant assessment of the surviving output: naive tomography can create or overlook errors during measurement, yielding an artificially flattering fidelity. The team therefore developed an appropriate measurement protocol to bound the magic-state quality under the experiment's assumptions. The result is a laboratory test of a specific protected-state preparation path, not a numerical simulation or an unimplemented architectural proposal. [1] [2]

The published headline numbers must be kept together. Cultivation reduced error by a factor of 40 relative to the team's injection baseline, and the retained states had fidelity at least 0.9999(1). Only about 8% of attempts were retained. Here fidelity is the probability-like overlap of the prepared quantum state with its target, while the parenthesized digit denotes uncertainty in the final digits as reported. It would be misleading to say the entire quantum computer became 40 times more reliable, or that 99.99% of all preparation attempts succeeded. The conditional quality of accepted outputs is high precisely because the protocol filters the attempts. [1] [2]

A simple accounting example reveals the trade-off. If every attempt independently succeeds with probability p = 0.08, the mean number of attempts per accepted state is 1/p = 12.5. For 100 requested accepted outputs a simple idealized throughput estimate is about 1,250 attempts, before the cost of cooling, decoding, state expansion, discarded run time, or contention with other processor tasks. That calculation is derived solely from the reported retention fraction; it is not the authors' measured wall-clock production rate. A factory comparison needs the qubit footprint times active cycles per delivered usable state, not acceptance probability or final fidelity in isolation. [1] [2]

Why does expansion into a surface code matter? Code distance describes how many physical errors must accumulate, in a simplified adversarial picture, before an undetected logical failure becomes possible. A small initial code offers cheaper state injection and checking, while a larger surface-code patch offers more protection during later storage and use. The difficulty is moving the logical information between codes without introducing unprotected operations. The experimental report explicitly includes this transition, making the result more than a demonstration of error-detecting initialization. The article's accessible summary does not establish a full running universal algorithm that consumes the output in a deep logical circuit. [1] [2]

The broader architectural comparison remains open. State distillation has mature analysis and can be massively parallelized; cultivation may reduce resource overhead in favorable noise and architecture regimes but consumes many failed runs. Relevant baselines include direct injection, one or more distillation levels, time-to-delivery, logical output error conditional on acceptance and full hardware occupancy. The Science paper published on October 15, 2026, updates the status of a proposal already on arXiv since December 2025 from preprint to peer-reviewed experimental evidence. That chronology is more informative than treating a recent press mention as the invention of magic states. [1] [2]

## ESP32 · Real-time firmware and graphics — An ESP32-S3 lighting system that prefers a dropped frame to a stale one

The new open-source release explains how to move browser-rendered images over Wi-Fi into DMA-timed LEDs—and what its WLED comparison really measures.

r/esp32 author project release posted Wednesday 21 October 2026 (public search; direct Reddit page inaccessible); original genbs/reactive-leds repository, firmware, data protocol and measurements inspected 22 October. ESP-IDF v5.5.x project; author's results not independently reproduced.

The most interesting part of this week's ESP32 community material is a project that makes an end-to-end latency decision explicit. On October 21 an r/esp32 author published the source of Reactive-LEDS, previously shown as a collection of LED fixtures for live-coded performances. The system turns colors sampled from a computer-generated image into light on one-meter tubes. Its software path is browser canvas to JavaScript client, local WebSocket proxy, Wi-Fi UDP packets, ESP32-S3 protocol task, hardware RMT transmitter and physical LED strip. Each boundary was chosen to prevent the display from gradually falling behind the current animation, rather than to guarantee that every network packet arrives. [1] [2] [5]

The exact hardware constrains the visual resolution. Each one-meter tube uses a 24-volt flexible-chip-on-board strip with roughly 896 individual light emitters, but only sixteen separately controlled IC groups per meter. The controller therefore updates sixteen segments, not 896 independent pixels. Its logical color format is four bytes per segment in W,R,G,B order. An ESP32-S3 board drives the strip through GPIO18 and a 330-ohm series data resistor; an XL4015 step-down converter provides 5 volts to the controller from the common 24-volt source. Shared ground is essential for the logic signal to have a meaningful reference. Different strip types need both byte-order and encoder changes; a three-channel WS2812 strip is not the same as this RGBW device. [2] [3] [4]

The browser cannot send arbitrary UDP directly, so the supplied command-line application acts as a local WebSocket-to-UDP bridge. The JavaScript client accepts canvas image data and a user-defined quadrilateral mapping region; it samples a line through that region and sends color values for the physical segment positions. The mapping code uses reusable buffers and interpolation so each render need not allocate a new array for every lamp. The device's UDP port defaults to 4210, and it can be configured at run time after flashing. A useful abstraction emerges: screen geometry belongs on the host, while precisely timed serial waveform generation belongs on the microcontroller. [2] [3] [5]

At the microcontroller boundary the project chooses UDP rather than TCP because a missed frame is less disruptive than a late one. Its lwIP receive mailbox is deliberately limited to six packets. When the receiver cannot keep up, it drops new arrivals rather than retaining an unbounded backlog. Its author reports this drop-tail policy looks smoother for their installation than an aggressive queue drain that jumps over intermediate transitions. Neither policy can eliminate radio loss or congestion; both choose which visible defect is preferable. Wi-Fi and lwIP processing are pinned to ESP32-S3 CPU0, with the render/protocol task on CPU1. The configuration uses a one-millisecond FreeRTOS tick to make short polling delays meaningful. [2] [3]

For the LED waveform the firmware does not bit-bang timing-critical high and low pulses in a task loop. The ESP32-S3 RMT peripheral converts byte data into timed symbol transitions; its configured clock resolution is 10 megahertz, equivalent to 100 nanoseconds per tick. The checked source requests DMA, configures a transmit queue depth of four and a 64-symbol memory block, and keeps relevant interrupt routines safe in IRAM. Double buffers prevent the application from rewriting the byte array that the hardware is still transmitting. When the previous RMT transfer has not completed after a one-millisecond wait, the software increments a dropped-frame counter rather than queuing a stale physical update. [3] [4]

A small derived RAM calculation puts that design in context. Sixteen segments times four color bytes require 64 bytes for one logical frame; two such application buffers require 128 bytes, excluding RMT symbols, DMA descriptors, queues and the rest of the application. This is why the author notes that PSRAM is not necessary for the active LED path. For larger strips, memory and transmission time both grow with segment count. The protocol encodes segment index and total count in one byte each, so its advertised practical ceiling is 255 logical segments per device; it does not scale to an unlimited wall merely because Wi-Fi provides packets. [2] [3] [4]

The author also made an unusually useful apples-to-apples comparison against stock WLED on ESP32-S3 boards with sixteen segments. According to their README, both systems displayed 60-frame-per-second streams smoothly. At attempted 90 and 120 frames per second, stock WLED effectively coalesced changes into about 62 rendered frames per second, while this firmware rendered all updates in their test. This is an attributed result from the builder, not independent validation. The result applies to that configuration and workload, not every WLED output driver or ESP32 chip. At normal browser-refresh rates the same author says the practical difference is not significant, an important constraint on claims of superiority. [1] [2]

Provisioning is also part of the engineering story. USB serial and BLE both deliver credentials to the same configuration layer, and a single firmware image can receive per-device hostname, GPIO, segment count and port settings. However the documentation explicitly warns that BLE provisioning has neither pairing nor encryption and transfers Wi-Fi credentials in cleartext during setup. That is acceptable only within an appropriately controlled threat model. The project also disabled station roaming assistance because background channel scans produced visible interruptions for stationary lighting; this is not a universal optimization for moving clients. The reusable lesson is a system-level latency budget: isolate network work, bound queued data, leave tight timing to peripherals, expose dropped-frame counters and explain what is traded away for responsiveness. [2] [3] [5]
